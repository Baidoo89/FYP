# CHAPTER FIVE: SUMMARY, CONCLUSION, AND RECOMMENDATIONS

## 5.1 Introduction

This chapter presents a summary of the study, evaluates the implemented system against the objectives set out in Chapter One, states the conclusions drawn from the design, implementation, and testing process, and offers recommendations and suggestions for future work.

## 5.2 Summary of the Study

This project designed and implemented a Digital Staff Promotion Support System for Ghana Communication Technology University. The study began by identifying operational weaknesses in GCTU's existing, largely manual promotion process — limited application-status visibility, dependence on physical documentation, administrative delays from manual routing, inconsistent application of promotion criteria, and weak auditability (Chapter One). A review of relevant literature and existing systems (Interfolio, Watermark Faculty Success, Workday HCM, Oracle HCM, and the University of Ghana's promotion process) established that these problems are common to manual and semi-digitised promotion workflows, and confirmed a gap: no existing system was localised to GCTU's specific promotion structure, evidence categories, and institutional criteria (Chapter Two).

A prototype was then designed and built using the Prototyping methodology: a Next.js/TypeScript web application backed by a PostgreSQL (Neon) database through Prisma ORM, implementing role-based access control for five distinct roles, a document evidence-upload and verification workflow, a rule-based eligibility decision-support engine, audit logging, and reporting (Chapter Three, Chapter Four).

The completed system was tested against its own approved specification and, critically, exercised end-to-end in a live browser session covering the full workflow from lecturer submission through HOD review, HR verification, committee recommendation, and HR final decision. This testing surfaced and corrected a real defect in the eligibility engine before completion (Chapter Four, §4.9.2 and §4.13.5).

## 5.4 Answers to the Research Questions

Chapter One (§1.5) set out five research questions guiding this study. Each is answered below against the evidence produced by the design, implementation, and testing carried out in Chapters Three and Four.

**RQ1: What operational bottlenecks and data security risks exist within the traditional staff promotion framework currently utilised at GCTU?**
The manual process was found to rely on physical documentation with limited status visibility, department-to-department routing delays, inconsistent application of promotion criteria across reviewers, and weak auditability of who verified what evidence and when (Chapter One, §1.3; Chapter Three, §3.9–3.10). These findings, grounded in GCTU's own Basic Laws, Conditions of Service, and Administrative Procedures Manual, directly motivated the workflow, verification, and audit-logging design implemented in Chapter Four.

**RQ2: What foundational human resource and software engineering factors influence the successful implementation of digital promotion systems?**
The literature and existing-systems review (Chapter Two) identified role-based access control, workflow state management, evidence/document integrity, and auditability as the recurring software engineering factors, alongside HR factors such as transparent criteria and consistent application of promotion rules (Armstrong and Taylor, 2017; Aguinis, 2013). The implemented system operationalises these directly: centrally enforced RBAC (§4.8), a validated workflow state machine (§4.5), a document/verification data model separating uploaded evidence from verification decisions (§4.4), and append-only audit logging (§4.4, §4.13.5).

**RQ3: How can digital technologies and automated evaluation improve the reliability and objectivity of promotion outcomes?**
The eligibility engine (§4.9) applies the same configured criteria to every application, removing the variation that comes from different reviewers manually checking evidence completeness by hand. This is a meaningful reliability improvement for the specific thing it checks — verified evidence-category completeness against configured weights — but, consistent with the answer to Objective 5 above, it does not itself make the qualitative judgement of teaching, research, or service quality; that judgement remains with HOD/Dean, HR, and the Committee. The honest answer is therefore that automation improves objectivity in the mechanical, rule-checkable part of the process, while the qualitative assessment — where subjectivity genuinely belongs to human academic judgement — is intentionally left to the university's reviewers (§1.8).

**RQ4: What functional requirements and module features should a modern promotion system include to satisfy GCTU staff requirements?**
The system modules table (§4.10) and the five role-specific portals (§4.12) answer this directly: authentication and onboarding, promotion-request and evidence-upload workflows, department-level academic review, HR verification, an eligibility/decision-support engine, committee review, audit logging, notifications, and reporting/analytics. These map to the functional requirements set out in Chapter Three and were each exercised in end-to-end testing (§4.13.5).

**RQ5: How can automation and real-time tracking enhance structural transparency and institutional accountability?**
The Status History and Audit Trail (§4.4, §4.13.5) give every application an immutable, timestamped record of who took each action and when, visible to the applicant and to HR/Committee reviewers, and the notification system informs the applicant and the next responsible role as soon as a status changes. This was confirmed correct across a full application lifecycle from Draft through Completed during testing, directly addressing the transparency and accountability weaknesses identified under RQ1.

## 5.5 Conclusion

The study set out to determine whether a digital staff promotion support system could meaningfully improve the transparency, accountability, and efficiency of GCTU's promotion process without displacing the university's statutory decision-making authorities. The completed and tested system demonstrates that this is achievable: evidence submission, verification, eligibility recommendation, committee review, and final administrative decision are now digitally tracked and auditable end-to-end, while every actual promotion decision remains with GCTU's human reviewers and authorities, consistent with the delimitation stated in Chapter One (§1.8) — the system supports the process, it does not replace it.

The project also demonstrates, concretely, why testing a running system matters more than reviewing its code in isolation: the eligibility engine's defect (§4.9.2) was invisible from a static reading of the scoring rules and would not have been caught without deliberately exercising the full application through a real browser session across all five roles.

A further conclusion from the implementation is that the strongest value of the system is not only the eligibility score, but the structure it gives to the promotion process. By separating application submission, departmental academic review, HR evidence verification, committee recommendation, authority approval, and final close-out into distinct logged stages, the system reduces ambiguity about responsibility. This separation is important in a university context because promotion decisions involve both administrative completeness and academic judgement; the implemented system supports both by preserving evidence, comments, status history, and audit records without pretending that a software score can replace the statutory review bodies.

## 5.6 Limitations of the Implemented System

Consistent with the delimitations declared in Chapter One (§1.8–§1.9), the implemented prototype has the following known limitations:

1. **Eligibility scoring is completeness-based, not quality-based.** The engine scores an application by which required evidence categories (Teaching, Research, Service) have been verified as present, using the fixed weights and performance bands defined in the approved proposal. It does not implement GCTU's full qualitative assessment cascade — where the Head of Department, Faculty Appointments and Promotions Committee, and University Appointments and Promotions Committee each independently rate an applicant's teaching, research, and service as Excellent/Very Good/Good/Satisfactory/Unsatisfactory (per the GCTU Heads of Academic Departments Handbook, 2025) — because the approved scope defines the system as a rule-based decision-support tool operating on verified evidence, not a replacement for that human judgement (§1.8).
2. **No dedicated committee-scoring interface.** Committee reviewers record a recommendation (Recommended / Not Recommended / Requires Further Review) and a comment; they do not enter a separate numeric or qualitative score per evidence category.
3. **Administrative dashboards are not yet optimised for mobile information density.** Pages combining a request queue with a full record detail (HR Requests, Committee Review) render correctly and without layout breakage on mobile, but as one long single-column scroll rather than a mobile-specific list-then-detail navigation pattern.
4. **Real institutional data was not used.** As stated in Chapter One (§1.9), promotion records contain sensitive personal and professional information; the system was seeded and tested with representative sample data rather than actual GCTU staff records.
5. **No automated regression test suite.** Verification to date has been static analysis (TypeScript, production build), a database health check, and manual/scripted end-to-end browser testing rather than a maintained automated test suite that would catch regressions on future changes.
6. **An unresolved citation-verification gap.** The eligibility performance-band table in Chapter Three (§3.17) is cited to the GCTU Basic Laws (Schedule J), which the project team was not able to independently confirm page-by-page during this study. This is disclosed here explicitly rather than presented as a fully verified citation, and should be confirmed directly against Schedule J before any institutional adoption.
7. **Formal user acceptance testing was not completed against the current build.** Chapter Three (§3.19) proposed UAT with representative users; the task script and feedback form for this were prepared (Chapter Four, §4.13.7), but the exercise itself had not been run with real participants at the time of writing. Functional correctness was instead established through the test cases in Chapter Four, Table 4.1, and the end-to-end workflow test (§4.13.5), which are evidence of the system working as specified but are not a substitute for independent user feedback on usability.

## 5.7 Recommendations

Recommendations here concern GCTU's institutional adoption and organisational implementation of the system, as distinct from the technical extensions listed as future work in §5.8.

1. **To GCTU management and the HR Directorate**: the prototype demonstrates sufficient functional completeness to justify a supervised pilot with a small number of real (consenting) applications before any wider rollout, so that the eligibility engine's completeness-based scoring can be validated against actual committee outcomes before it informs any live promotion decision.
2. **To GCTU management**: commission a formal policy-validation exercise involving HR, at least one HOD/Dean, and a Committee representative to confirm the configured criteria (required evidence categories, weights, minimum years in rank) match current institutional policy before the criteria are relied upon operationally, since these are configurable and were set during this study from the approved proposal rather than a live policy sign-off.
3. **To GCTU IT governance**: establish data-governance rules for promotion evidence (retention period, access logging review cadence, backup policy) and approve secure hosting arrangements before any pilot uses real staff data, given the sensitivity of promotion records noted in §1.9.
4. **To GCTU management**: budget for brief role-specific training for HR, HOD/Dean, and Committee users before a pilot begins; the workflow is standard web-application navigation, but the distinction between department-level academic review and HR's official verification (§4.12) should be explained explicitly so reviewers do not treat the two as interchangeable.

## 5.8 Suggestions for Future Work

1. **Qualitative committee scoring module.** Extend the eligibility engine with a structured interface allowing the Head of Department, Faculty Appointments and Promotions Committee, and University Appointments and Promotions Committee to each record a qualitative rating (Excellent/Very Good/Good/Satisfactory/Unsatisfactory) per assessable area, combined according to GCTU's actual per-rank rules (e.g., Senior Lecturer requires "Excellent" in Teaching and Research plus at least "Satisfactory" in Service, or "Good" in all three), rather than the current document-completeness proxy.
2. **Full Schedule K coverage.** Extend the configurable criteria and evidence-category model to the administrative and professional staff promotion requirements under Schedule K, which were outside the academic-pathway scope demonstrated in this prototype (§4.9.1).
3. **Mobile-optimised administrative views.** Redesign the HR Requests, Committee Review, and Analytics pages for mobile with a list-then-detail navigation pattern (or collapsible sections) instead of a single stacked column, reducing scroll length on smaller screens.
4. **Automated regression testing.** Introduce an automated end-to-end test suite (e.g., Playwright) covering the workflow paths that were manually verified during this study, so future changes are checked automatically rather than requiring another manual pass.
5. **Integration with GCTU's official systems.** As noted in Chapter One (§1.9), full institutional deployment would require integration with GCTU's official staff database and single sign-on, which was out of scope for this prototype.
6. **Database cleanup.** The `Score` entity defined in the Prisma schema (Chapter Four, §4.4) is no longer used by the eligibility engine following the correction in §4.9.2; a future migration should either remove it or repurpose it to store the per-category qualitative ratings suggested in item 1 above.
7. **Developer tooling.** Adopt the ESLint configuration Next.js offers out of the box (currently absent from the project) before any further UI work, to catch accessibility and correctness issues automatically rather than only through manual review.
8. **External-assessor and digital-signature support.** Extend the workflow to support external assessors and digitally signed committee/authority decisions, strengthening the audit trail for the final approval step beyond the current in-system role-based recording.

## 5.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter summarised the study, answered the five research questions set out in Chapter One against the evidence produced by the implemented system, showed that all six objectives were achieved (five fully, and the eligibility-engine objective within its declared prototype scope), drew conclusions about the value of testing a running system rather than reviewing code alone, stated the limitations that follow directly from the approved project scope, and separated institutional recommendations from technical future work — most notably, extending the current completeness-based eligibility engine toward GCTU's full qualitative assessment process, covering Schedule K, and improving the administrative interface for mobile use.
